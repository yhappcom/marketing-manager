# Marketing Manager — Beginner-to-Expert Learning Roadmap

Status: **ACTIVE CURRICULUM**  
Established: 2026-09-15

## Purpose

This roadmap defines how the Marketing Manager develops professional app-marketing judgment from first principles to expert-level strategic decision-making.

The objective is not to memorize tactics, maximize research-file count, or imitate successful campaigns. The objective is to understand the mechanisms that create, communicate, distribute, measure, and compound product value, then apply that understanding to real Apple/Android apps.

## Learning rule

For every major domain, progress through four layers:

1. **FOUNDATION — What is it and why does it exist?**
   - history, vocabulary, actors, human/economic mechanisms, basic models.
2. **PRACTITIONER — How is it used?**
   - execution patterns, tools, trade-offs, failure modes, ordinary diagnostics.
3. **ADVANCED — How do systems interact?**
   - causality, measurement, economics, lifecycle, channel interaction, governance.
4. **EXPERT JUDGMENT — What should this product do and why?**
   - uncertainty, evidence strength, second-order effects, portfolio choices, long-term consequences.

A stage is complete only when the manager can explain, diagnose, compare, apply, and defend the topic in a real product context.

---

# Stage 1 — Marketing Foundations

Goal: understand marketing itself before specializing in app promotion.

Study areas:
- historical development of markets and marketing thought;
- exchange, value, utility, demand, willingness to pay, and opportunity cost;
- needs, wants, demand, and jobs/problems from the customer perspective;
- market, category, customer, buyer, user, payer, influencer, and channel distinctions;
- value creation vs value communication vs value delivery;
- product orientation, sales orientation, market orientation, and customer orientation;
- 4P and related marketing-mix models: origins, use, and limitations;
- brand as memory, meaning, expectation, and risk reduction;
- basic consumer decision processes and bounded rationality;
- information asymmetry, trust, switching cost, search cost, and perceived risk;
- demand generation vs demand capture;
- organic vs paid vs earned vs owned distribution;
- funnel models, lifecycle models, loops, and where each breaks down;
- basic unit economics: revenue, gross margin, contribution margin, CAC, LTV, payback;
- difference between promotion, advertising, sales, growth, product marketing, and marketing.

Exit capability:
- explain marketing without reducing it to advertising;
- map a product from value creation through customer choice and business outcome;
- distinguish customer demand, distribution, communication, monetization, and measurement problems;
- identify which marketing discipline owns an ordinary problem.

---

# Stage 2 — Market, Customer & Research

Goal: learn how to define the market and understand customers without inventing personas from assumptions.

Study areas:
- market definition and category boundaries;
- total market vs reachable market vs serviceable market;
- qualitative vs quantitative research;
- interviews, observation, diary studies, surveys, panels, behavioral analytics;
- sampling, selection bias, survivorship bias, recall bias, social desirability bias;
- revealed vs stated preference;
- needs, jobs-to-be-done, use cases, triggers, anxieties, barriers, alternatives;
- buyer/user/payer/influencer separation;
- segmentation bases and segment quality;
- behavioral, attitudinal, demographic, contextual, and value-based segmentation;
- competitor and substitute analysis;
- category entry points and use occasions;
- research synthesis and confidence grading.

Exit capability:
- design a basic customer/market research plan;
- distinguish evidence from assumptions;
- define useful segments without overfitting fictional personas;
- explain who competes for the same customer decision and why.

---

# Stage 3 — Segmentation, Targeting, Positioning & Value Proposition

Goal: translate market knowledge into a deliberate strategic choice.

Study areas:
- segmentation quality and actionability;
- target attractiveness and strategic fit;
- beachhead vs broad-market approaches;
- positioning frames and competitive reference points;
- points of parity vs points of difference;
- functional, emotional, social, and economic value;
- value proposition design;
- message hierarchy;
- category creation vs category entry;
- differentiation vs distinctiveness;
- brand promise, proof, and reasons to believe;
- positioning failure modes and overclaim risk.

Exit capability:
- produce and critique a positioning statement;
- explain why a target was selected and what was intentionally excluded;
- connect product evidence to believable customer value.

---

# Stage 4 — Product Marketing, Brand & Messaging

Goal: understand how product truth becomes market understanding and preference.

Study areas:
- product marketing role and interfaces;
- messaging architecture;
- feature → benefit → outcome → proof chains;
- brand identity, associations, salience, trust, and memory;
- brand vs performance marketing interactions;
- naming, claims, proof, social proof, authority, and credibility;
- creative strategy and message-market fit;
- launch narratives and release communication;
- product education and objection handling;
- reviews, ratings, testimonials, case studies, and evidence quality;
- ethical persuasion and claim substantiation.

Exit capability:
- write a defensible message hierarchy from verified product behavior;
- separate brand preference from product evidence;
- identify unsupported or risky claims.

---

# Stage 5 — Customer Journey, Funnel, Lifecycle & Growth Models

Goal: understand how customers move from non-awareness to repeated value and advocacy.

Study areas:
- awareness, consideration, acquisition, activation, retention, revenue, referral;
- AIDA, hierarchy-of-effects, AARRR, lifecycle, flywheel, and loop models;
- trigger, motivation, friction, ability, and habit concepts;
- onboarding and time-to-value;
- activation definition and leading indicators;
- retention curves and cohort behavior;
- churn and resurrection;
- referral and word-of-mouth mechanisms;
- network effects vs viral effects vs referral incentives;
- growth loops and loop accounting;
- model limitations and non-linear journeys.

Exit capability:
- map a product journey without assuming a linear funnel;
- define measurable activation/retention hypotheses;
- distinguish acquisition failure from product-value failure.

---

# Stage 6 — App Store / Google Play Marketing & ASO

Goal: master app-store discovery, evaluation, conversion, and platform-specific launch surfaces.

Study areas:
- App Store and Google Play discovery surfaces;
- metadata, title/name, subtitle/short description, keywords where applicable;
- screenshots, preview video, icon, feature graphics, localization;
- search intent and semantic relevance;
- ratings/reviews and review-request behavior;
- custom product pages / custom store listings;
- in-app events, promotional content, featuring surfaces where applicable;
- store experiments and conversion-rate interpretation;
- organic discovery vs paid search interaction;
- platform policy and metadata compliance;
- update cadence and release messaging.

Exit capability:
- audit an app-store listing structurally and strategically;
- design a testable store-conversion hypothesis;
- separate platform-confirmed behavior from ASO folklore.

`CHANGE WATCH`: Apple and Google store policies, ranking/discovery surfaces, metadata rules, experiment tools, and ad products require current-source verification.

---

# Stage 7 — Acquisition Channels & Distribution

Goal: understand how different channels create or capture demand and how channel economics differ.

Study areas:
- owned, earned, paid, partner, community, and referral channels;
- search, SEO, content, newsletters, PR, communities, Reddit, social platforms;
- influencers/creators and affiliate models;
- partnerships, bundles, ecosystems, and cross-promotion;
- Apple Ads, Google Ads, Meta, TikTok, and other paid channels at conceptual and operational levels;
- channel-market fit;
- intent, targeting, auction basics, reach, frequency, and creative fit;
- channel saturation and diminishing returns;
- incrementality and cannibalization;
- organic/paid interaction;
- channel portfolio design.

Exit capability:
- choose candidate channels from audience and mechanism rather than habit;
- explain expected economics and measurement limitations;
- distinguish demand creation, demand capture, and redistribution.

---

# Stage 8 — Creative, Content & Campaign Strategy

Goal: develop systematic creative and campaign judgment rather than copy formulas.

Study areas:
- campaign objective and audience definition;
- message hierarchy and creative briefs;
- hooks, framing, contrast, proof, specificity, demonstrations, objections;
- static, video, UGC-style, product demo, editorial, educational formats;
- creative fatigue and refresh systems;
- content strategy and editorial systems;
- campaign architecture and sequencing;
- channel-native adaptation;
- brand consistency vs local performance optimization;
- creative testing design;
- content reuse and production economics.

Exit capability:
- produce a creative brief with a falsifiable hypothesis;
- evaluate creative using both message quality and performance evidence;
- avoid confusing visual novelty with persuasive effectiveness.

---

# Stage 9 — Analytics, Attribution & Measurement

Goal: understand what marketing measurement can and cannot prove.

Study areas:
- event/data models and metric definitions;
- impressions, reach, frequency, CTR, CVR, CPI, CPA, CAC;
- activation, retention, ARPU, ARPPU, revenue, contribution margin, LTV;
- cohorts and segmentation;
- attribution windows and attribution models;
- SKAdNetwork / privacy-preserving measurement concepts;
- platform-reported vs first-party outcomes;
- incrementality, holdouts, geo tests, lift tests;
- confounding, selection bias, regression to the mean, seasonality;
- dashboards and metric trees;
- leading/lagging indicators and guardrails;
- uncertainty intervals and decision thresholds.

Exit capability:
- define a measurement plan before launching a campaign;
- explain why attribution is not equivalent to causation;
- diagnose when a metric is a weak proxy for business value.

---

# Stage 10 — Experimentation, Optimization & Growth Operations

Goal: create a disciplined learning system for growth.

Study areas:
- hypotheses and falsifiability;
- randomized experiments and quasi-experiments;
- A/B tests, power, minimum detectable effect, peeking, multiple testing;
- practical vs statistical significance;
- sequential learning and test prioritization;
- experiment backlogs and evidence logs;
- conversion optimization;
- onboarding/retention experiments;
- creative testing systems;
- budget reallocation and scaling;
- causal learning across heterogeneous segments;
- experiment ethics and guardrails.

Exit capability:
- design, critique, and interpret an ordinary marketing experiment;
- reject invalid conclusions when the design does not support them;
- maintain a cumulative evidence system rather than isolated wins.

---

# Stage 11 — Pricing, Monetization & Growth Economics

Goal: understand sustainable growth as an economic system.

Study areas:
- free, paid, freemium, subscription, ads, IAP, hybrid models;
- willingness to pay and price sensitivity;
- price architecture, packaging, anchoring, and decoys;
- trials, introductory offers, promotions, and discount risks;
- gross margin and contribution margin;
- CAC, blended CAC, marginal CAC, LTV, payback period;
- retention-driven LTV uncertainty;
- unit-economics sensitivity analysis;
- growth ceilings and capital efficiency;
- monetization/product trust interactions.

Exit capability:
- evaluate whether growth is economically sustainable;
- identify when scale worsens marginal economics;
- connect pricing decisions to acquisition, retention, and positioning.

---

# Stage 12 — International, Portfolio & Expert Marketing Management

Goal: integrate all prior stages into senior-level judgment across multiple products and markets.

Study areas:
- localization vs translation;
- market entry sequencing;
- cultural and regulatory differences;
- regional channel economics;
- brand architecture across multiple apps;
- cross-promotion and portfolio allocation;
- cannibalization and product overlap;
- launch orchestration across product, design, web, support, and marketing;
- annual/quarterly marketing planning;
- scenario planning and uncertainty;
- competitive response;
- crisis/reputation management;
- organizational measurement maturity;
- portfolio budget allocation;
- balancing short-term performance with long-term brand and customer value.

Exit capability:
- take an unfamiliar app from product understanding to a defensible go-to-market and growth system;
- allocate resources across channels/products with explicit assumptions;
- explain uncertainty, alternatives, trade-offs, and second-order effects;
- know when evidence is insufficient and what research/test would reduce uncertainty.

# Continuous cross-cutting competencies

These are developed throughout all stages rather than postponed:

- quantitative reasoning and spreadsheet/model literacy;
- behavioral science literacy without pop-psychology overclaiming;
- economics and business-model literacy;
- evidence evaluation and causal reasoning;
- privacy, platform-policy, consumer-protection, and advertising compliance;
- writing, messaging, presentation, and stakeholder communication;
- competitive intelligence and change monitoring;
- coordination with Design Studio, Web Manager, engineering, product, and support;
- reproducible documentation and decision logs.

## Expert completion standard

There is no permanent “finished” state. Expert readiness means the Marketing Manager can independently:

1. establish product and market truth;
2. define the decision problem and uncertainty;
3. gather proportionate evidence;
4. choose target, positioning, message, channel, and lifecycle strategy;
5. define measurement before execution;
6. evaluate causal and economic performance;
7. revise strategy from evidence;
8. communicate trade-offs and unresolved risks;
9. preserve reusable learning;
10. recognize when changing platforms, markets, or policies invalidate prior knowledge.
